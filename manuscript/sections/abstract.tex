\begin{abstract}
We test whether a local residual correction confirmed in an earlier chemistry study transfers to composition-based materials property prediction.
The pipeline has two stages:
\begin{itemize}
\item cross-fitted stacking of diverse experts;
\item a novelty-damped local out-of-fold residual correction.
\end{itemize}
The test uses the four composition tasks of Matbench v0.1 (steels, expt\_gap, expt\_is\_metal, glass) under a strict protocol:
\begin{itemize}
\item only the official folds are used;
\item every model, hyperparameter and design choice is made by cross-validation inside each training fold;
\item the analysis plan was written, and the code frozen by SHA-256 hash, before any test fold was scored;
\item every task, fold and comparison is reported.
\end{itemize}
The simplex-constrained stack never lost to an equal-weight ensemble and improved calibration. Its gains over the best single expert were small (largest on glass).
The local correction improved one of the four tasks under the pre-registered rule. On expt\_gap the mean absolute error fell from \GapStack{} to \GapStackLocal{}~eV (\RelGap\%, 5/5 folds, Holm-adjusted $p=\PHolmGap$).
It had no detectable effect on steels (\RelSteels\%), and its own nested selection declined it on the two classification tasks.
Stratifying by distance to the nearest training composition shows that the correction hurts for near-duplicate compositions, where the deployed stack has already absorbed the neighbours' errors. A fold-matched variant designed to remove this double counting did not help.
Among the leaderboard entries the method would rank \SteelsRank{}, \GapRank{}, \MetalRank{} and \GlassRank{} on steels, expt\_gap, expt\_is\_metal and glass. It does not beat the best entry on any task.
We also show that most leaderboard classification scores are label-based balanced accuracies, which are not comparable with probability ROC-AUCs.
\end{abstract}
